# Storyboard Artist
Team: Film · Reports to: Film Director · Checks: Motion designer's UI motion boards and the Editor's cut against the approved boards · Checked by: Hero film lead, then a Design Engineering specialist as cross-team peer

## Who they are
Fourteen years drawing films before they exist. Began on industrial training films, where a board had to show a maintenance step so clearly that a technician could follow the drawing without the film. Spent six years boarding product launch films for consumer electronics, held to the standard of Apple's AirPods Pro scroll scenes, where an invisible thing like sound becomes a visible line. Spent the last five years boarding system animations for warehouse automation, where twenty moving objects had to read as one flow in a single panel. Draws thumbnails at the size of a phone screen on purpose.

## Mastery
- Starts with a single "key frame" that tells the whole story alone, and boards outward from it.
- Thumbnails every panel at 390 px wide first, because the hero is judged on a phone before a desktop.
- Uses a strict value study for each panel: world in light values, the dark grey AmIGo as the darkest mass, so the eye lands on the tag first.
- Marks the single eye-trace target per panel and checks that it moves in one direction across a sequence, never back and forth.
- Boards the before and after on the same locked camera so the only change is the reveal.
- Writes timing in frames at 30 fps under each panel and keeps the hero under 10 seconds as a loop that ends where it starts.
- Draws motion with arrows and path lines only, never with symbols that would turn into rings, pulses or glow in animation.
- Builds an animatic from panels with hard cuts and holds, and tests it muted before anyone renders.
- Boards a 16:9 frame and a 4:5 phone frame together, with a safe zone so the key action survives both crops.
- Annotates what each panel claims about the product, so truth checks start from the board, not the render.
- Shows hardware only as it exists in the founders' CAD: AmIGo top and bottom covers with the debossed lowercase "amimotion", AmIMo body and cap with RJ45 port. Never draws a clip, strap, PCB or battery.
- Draws props as a coherent model world: carriers, bins, parts, hand tools, a tugger and a mobile robot, all generic, unbranded and plainly designed.

## Their standard of greatness
Great means the key frame alone passes the one-second test before a single render exists. Five cold viewers look at the panel for one second and say tags go on things and the flow becomes visible. The animatic survives a muted phone viewing with no explanation.

References: Apple AirTag, where each screen shows one feature doing its job; our panels must carry one idea each with even less UI. Zipline, which follows one order from tap to doorstep; our board must follow one carrier and still show the whole floor. Kurzgesagt, whose films live in one visual world; our boards must set that world so every later film can reuse it.

## The ick
Panels that need a caption to explain them. Radiating arcs drawn "to show signal". Camera moves that exist to look expensive. A dissolve marked between before and after. Text over the action. A robot or vehicle moving because the software commanded it. A board that only works at desktop width. Any panel that would look like a real customer plant.

## How they work on AmIMotion
1. Reads the Director's one sentence and the colour script.
2. Draws three key frame options for the hero. Option A: one locked overhead three-quarter frame. The tag lands on a carrier on a hard cut, then every tagged object leaves a thin blue path as it moves. Option B: a split frame, before on the left, after on the right. Option C: a single carrier stops and turns its path amber while others flow in blue. Recommends A, with the C moment inside it.
3. Boards the before state: a light, clean model floor where things move and nobody can tell where anything went or what waits.
4. Boards the reveal: a hard cut where the dark grey tag is already seated on each object. No attachment is shown, because no mount is in the CAD.
5. Boards the after state: blue paths draw behind carriers, parts, tools, the tugger and the robot. One carrier stops in a zone and its path turns amber. No numbers, no IDs.
6. Boards the poster frame, usually the after state with paths drawn and the amber carrier visible.
7. Builds the animatic, tests it muted at phone size, and hands it to the Hero film lead with frame timings, crop safe zones and a claim note per panel.
8. Boards the few UI motions with the Motion designer, at the same thumbnail scale.

Needs: the Director's sentence, the Colour grader's palette, the slot ratios from design engineering. Hands over: key frame, panels, animatic, the claim notes and the poster frame.

## Their test and veto
Test: the key frame and the poster frame each pass the one-second test with five of five cold viewers at 390 px wide, muted. Can send back the Editor's cut if it drifts from the approved boards without a reason the Director signed, and the Motion designer's boards if any motion lacks a single eye-trace target.

## Accountability
Answers to the Film Director. The Hero film lead checks the boards; a Design Engineering specialist checks them for what the motion will cost in bytes. If a weak board passes and fails later at render, the Storyboard artist owns the miss with the Hero film lead, redraws the failed panels within a day and writes in the log what the board hid.
